The legal landscape that creates these ideas

Six laws and one protected name are changing what small companies must prove between now and 2029. Each turns a vague good practice into a documented obligation, and documented obligations are what small businesses pay to outsource.

Guardian — backup evidence and exposure evidence, monthly

Every company runs backups and has a firewall; almost none can prove the backups would restore after ransomware, or knows what of its equipment is reachable from the internet. Guardian connects read-only to the backup system, scans the customer's authorised addresses against known-vulnerable and end-of-life equipment, and Claude writes a plain-language monthly report plus a quarterly attestation. It is the evidence NIS2 auditors, cyber insurers and customers' supply-chain reviews now demand.

Product siteBusiness planLaunch planProject officeReview and plan v2 (3/10, MSP-first)

Why now

  • NIS2 names backup management as an evidenced control, and supply-chain clauses push the questions to small suppliers.
  • Insurers check tested restores within 90 days and immutable copies at renewal.
  • The Cyber Resilience Act made vulnerability reporting mandatory from 11 September 2026 and brings minimum five-year security-update periods from December 2027; unsupported equipment becomes a documented liability.
  • 12 October 2026: attackers reached refrigeration controllers at about 180 Intermarché stores and other French chains through remote-management software that could no longer be updated; losses in the tens of millions of euros. The exposure report exists to catch exactly that.

Offer

  • Company, €199 a month: backup evidence, non-intrusive exposure check, email spoofing protection (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), certificate and domain expiry, equipment end-of-life watch, monthly report, alerts, quarterly attestation.
  • Company Plus, €299 a month: adds leaked staff credentials, lookalike-domain watch, Microsoft 365 posture (read-only) and website hygiene.
  • Compliance, €399 a month: adds a NIS2 Article 21 policy kit and questionnaire answers generated from live evidence.
  • Provider, from €449 a month: IT providers, up to ten client sites, reports in their name.
  • No penetration testing. Public data and read-only connections only; Guardian observes and proves, it never attacks.

€1,000 a month is five Company customers. Thirty companies and ten providers is about €125,000 a year at roughly 85% gross margin. Clear employer side-business rules before the first sale.

Passport — digital product passports for small brands

The EU will require a scannable Digital Product Passport on textiles and then on most physical products. Enterprise tools cost thousands a month; a maker with 200 products a year cannot buy them. Passport is a €39-a-month service: upload a photo and answer a few questions, and Claude drafts the material, origin, care and repair data in the EU's structure, generates a QR or NFC page per product, and keeps the record for the product's life. It is the same tool ÞEL needs for its provenance tag.

Why now

  • The EU passport registry went live on 20 July 2026; the textile act is due in late 2027, with obligations around 2028–29.
  • Early adopters sell on the story before it is mandatory; ÞEL is customer number one.
  • The data model is published, so the product can be built before the act lands and adjusted when it does.

Offer and price

  • Maker, €39 a month: up to 300 products, QR pages, NFC export.
  • Brand, €149 a month: 3,000 products, batch import, custom domain.
  • Setup, €290: Claude-assisted data migration from a spreadsheet.

€1,000 a month is about 25 makers, which takes longer to find than seven MSPs: a slower, bigger idea.

MarketSmall textile and craft brands selling into the EUTens of thousands of small labels; passports become mandatory for textiles around 2028–29, then furniture and more.

The seven pillars

  • 1. Ask KAT. Type any idea ("buy the pullback just before the 30-minute close", "is the CPI move a sure bet?", "should I add to winners?") and get a plain answer from 15 years of minute data: does it work, how often, after costs, and why. Built from the tests KAT already runs; on 14 October five such questions were answered in minutes each.
  • 2. Why did the engine do that? Every engine decision explained live: what it saw, which rule allowed or blocked it, what happened next. Beginners learn by watching a disciplined process think.
  • 3. Learning paths for stocks, options and futures. Short lessons, each ending in a simulator task, from "what is a tick" to building and testing a first strategy.
  • 4. Simulators that behave like the real thing: real contracts and prices, realistic fills, server-side stops, with a coach after every trade.
  • 5. Training wheels. The risk rules KAT learned the hard way, built into every account: daily loss limit, one trade at a time at the open, a profit lock that switches to careful mode, a cost check before every trade.
  • 6. The library of what does not work. Every failed test as a two-minute lesson with its data: chasing CPI moves, adding to winners, wider stops, trend-day rules.
  • 7. A track record, and signals only when earned. Every engine's forward results after costs, published with its failures. Signals are shown only for an engine that has passed its 12-month gate, with its full record beside every call, and only if they demonstrably help people.

AFL — Icelandic strength nutrition

Iceland has produced more World's Strongest Man winners per head than any country, and the whole world knows it. AFL is a strength and recovery line built on what Icelanders ate and did to be strong for a thousand years: skyr, fish, seaweed, moss, cold water and lifting stones. The strongmen are the proof; the land is the product.

The product universe

Six families, about thirty products. Launch with three (marked ★), add the rest as each family proves demand. Every product has a real Icelandic ingredient, tradition or place behind it.

1. Protein and food

  • Skyr protein powder ★ — vanilla, bilberry and plain; Icelandic grass-fed whey blended with skyr cultures.
  • Harðfiskur protein snack — wind-dried cod, the original Icelandic strongman food at about 80% protein; sold in sports portions.
  • Cod protein and marine collagen — from Icelandic cod skin, zero-waste; powder and capsules.
  • Skyr protein bars — with bilberry, oats and Icelandic sea salt.
  • Free-range lamb jerky — Icelandic mountain lamb, nothing added but salt.
  • Söl (dulse) seaweed chips — the salty snack Icelanders have eaten since settlement; iron, iodine and umami.
  • Rúgbrauð carb bread — geothermally baked rye bread as the pre-training carb; sold frozen or as a bake-at-home mix.

2. Performance supplements

  • Electrolytes ★ — hand-harvested geothermal sea salt plus Icelandic kelp minerals.
  • Burnirót pre-workout — Rhodiola rosea, the golden root that grows wild in Iceland and was used by Vikings for stamina; with beet and bilberry.
  • Omega-3 — Icelandic cod liver oil, the recovery staple every Icelandic child grew up on.
  • Seaweed magnesium — marine magnesium for sleep and cramps.
  • Angelica and bilberry capsules — the Viking-era medicinal plant, from an Icelandic extractor.
  • Crowberry and bilberry gummies — Icelandic berries for antioxidants; the family-friendly entry product.

3. Recovery and the cold ritual

  • Icelandic moss recovery drink ★ — fjallagrös with honey and birch, the warm drink after the ice bath. Sold as tradition and ritual, no health claims.
  • Geothermal bath salts — mineral and silica salts for the hot-soak half of the hot-cold ritual.
  • Birch and moss muscle balm — Icelandic birch bark and moss in a warming rub.
  • Herbal teas — birch leaf, yarrow, angelica and moss blends.
  • Hot-cold ritual kit — timer, salts, moss drink and a þel wool towel in one box; the gift product.

4. Equipment

  • The Dritvík stones — cast replicas of the four lifting stones at Djúpalónssandur that fishermen had to lift to qualify for a boat, at the original weights: Amlóði 23 kg, Hálfdrættingur 54 kg, Hálfsterkur 100 kg, Fullsterkur 154 kg.
  • Húsafell stone — a replica of Iceland's most famous carry stone, about 186 kg, with its history.
  • Basalt-finish kettlebells and sandbags — cast iron finished like Icelandic lava rock.
  • Stone-lifting programme — a graded training plan from Amlóði to Fullsterkur, online, with a certificate when a filmed lift is verified.

5. Apparel, shared with ÞEL

  • Þel wool training knits and hoodies — the lopapeysa cut, made for lifting and for the walk from sauna to sea.
  • Salmon-leather lifting belt and wrist wraps — strong, thin, unmistakable; made from ÞEL's skins.
  • Cold-plunge robe and towel — in þel wool.

6. Experiences and content

  • Strongman camp in Iceland — four days: stone lifting on the beach, sea plunge, hot spring, Icelandic food; the highest-margin product in the line and the content engine for everything else.
  • Strongman history series — short films on Jón Páll's era, the stones and the fishermen, licensed carefully.
  • App — programmes, the stone ladder, cold-exposure log and reorders.

Manufacturing

No factory: a licensed EU contract manufacturer (GMP and HACCP certified) formulates, blends, lab-tests and fills under the AFL brand, the same route Grace uses. Minimum orders run from hundreds to a few thousand units per flavour. The Icelandic claim rests on Icelandic grass-fed whey or skyr powder from an Icelandic dairy, shipped to the EU blender; ask every supplier for batch traceability back to the dairy. If Icelandic whey volumes are too small, the honest fallback is skyr cultures and Icelandic ingredients on EU whey, stated plainly on the label.
